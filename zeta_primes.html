Two families of zeros

Trivial: −2, −4, −6, … on the real axis.

Non-trivial: inside the strip 0 < σ < 1. All the examples here lie on σ = ½, the critical line, with matching zeros below the real axis.

The Riemann hypothesis says every non-trivial zero lies on that line. Seeing these examples is not a proof.

What is ζ(s), and why isn’t this an ordinary graph?

For real inputs greater than 1, the zeta function begins as ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …. At s = 2, for example, its value is about 1.645.

Mathematicians extend this function to complex inputs, except for a pole at s = 1, where its size grows without bound. The series above only converges when σ > 1; this map uses the extended function elsewhere.

A complex input and a complex output would need four real coordinates. We show the two input coordinates as a map and use colour for the magnitude (distance of the output from zero). We do not show the output’s angle. Circle markers pinpoint known zeros that may be smaller than a pixel.

The map is calculated numerically, with exploration bounded to −12 ≤ σ ≤ 6 and −100 ≤ t ≤ 100. More about zeta zeros (NIST).

A staircase and a smooth guess

How many primes are there up to x?

The exact count, π(x), jumps by one at each prime. The logarithmic integral, li(x), follows its overall trend. Riemann’s R(x) refines that smooth estimate.

Why li(x)?

Near a large number x, primes have an average density of about 1 / ln x. Adding up that changing density gives a smooth estimate.

li′(x) = 1 / ln x

We use the standard starting value li(2) ≈ 1.045, then add the area under 1 / ln x from 2 onwards. Starting the count at zero instead would shift the curve down.

R(x) adjusts for prime powers such as 4, 8 and 9. It is still an estimate: being close does not mean it is exact, or always closer at every x.

Why are they waves?

A zero ½ + iγ gives a term involving:

cos(γ ln x)

The height γ sets how quickly it oscillates in ln x. Higher zeros add finer detail. Each pair also includes the matching zero ½ − iγ, so its combined contribution is real.

A finite sum is approximate. Ripples near a jump are expected. Extra waves need not improve every single point.

Does it ever become exact?

With all the zero pairs, in order of increasing height, and the fixed corrections included, the explicit formula recovers the prime count between the primes. At a prime p it gives the midpoint of the jump: π(p) − ½. Taking the value immediately to the right gives the usual π(p).

This display uses the first 100 pairs. Their positions are known; the full prime-counting formula does not depend on proving the Riemann hypothesis.

A little more maths: what is being added?

R(x) is a smooth estimate obtained by removing the extra counts from prime squares, cubes and higher powers. To keep the reconstruction faithful, we calculate through a weighted counter J: a prime counts 1, a prime square adds ½, a prime cube adds ⅓, and so on. Removing these extra counts gives π.

The exact starting point, with midpoint values at jumps, is:

J₀(x) = li(x) − 2 ∑γ > 0 Re Ei((½ + iγ) ln x) − ln 2 + ∫x∞ dt / [t(t² − 1) ln t]

We then combine J₀(x) − ½J₀(√x) − ⅓J₀(∛x) − ⅕J₀(x1/5) + ⅙J₀(x1/6) − ⅐J₀(x1/7). These are all the nonzero terms needed up to x = 200. Terms whose argument is below 2 tend to zero in the full formula; retaining the same terms across the graph keeps each finite reconstruction continuous.

The teal curve includes the constant and integral terms, as well as the prime-power adjustments to every wave. Its smooth starting part is close to, but not identical to, the dashed R(x) curve.
